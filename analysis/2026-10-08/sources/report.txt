Introduction
1.1 Problem and Motivation

Polypharmacy—the concurrent use of several medications by one patient—is common among older
adults and patients with chronic or complex diseases. Although often necessary, it raises the risk
of drug–drug interactions (DDIs), especially polypharmacy side eﬀects: adverse events that emerge
only when two or more drugs are taken together and cannot be attributed to a single drug. Such
interactions contribute to hospitalisation, morbidity, and post-market drug withdrawal; identifying
harmful combinations early is therefore a direct patient-safety concern.
    The problem is fundamentally combinatorial. With thousands of approved drugs, possible
combinations are too numerous for exhaustive clinical testing, and many interactions surface only
after market release. Computational screening is consequently essential. Drugs, their protein
targets, and protein interactions form a natural network, while a side eﬀect between drugs can
be represented as a typed edge. This project uses that perspective and the Decagon (BioSNAP)
dataset as its biomedical knowledge graph.

1.2 Related Work

1.2.1 What is already solid
Multi-relational link prediction on a drug–protein graph is a well-established and eﬀective formula-
tion. Decagon [1] introduced it, while TIP [2] substantially improved eﬃciency, reporting AUPRC
0.890 (a 7.2% improvement over Decagon), about 83× faster execution, and roughly threefold lower
memory use. Strong results have also been reported by molecular-structure approaches such as SSI-
DDI [3] and biomedical-knowledge-graph approaches including KnowDDI [4] and MKG-FENN [5].
    Most relevant here, a knowledge graph combined with a large language model (LLM) adds an
interpretable explanation layer to prediction. DDI-GPT [6] retrieves a subgraph around a drug
pair, converts it to text, and ﬁne-tunes an LLM to return both a prediction and a gene-level ra-
tionale. More broadly, large-scale evaluation indicates that even modest LLMs can predict DDIs
eﬀectively [7]. Recent systems extend this direction with mechanism-conditioned multi-agent rea-
soning (DDIAgents [9]) and dynamic multi-view graph-and-language reasoning (DrugReason [10]).

1.2.2 What is still missing
Three gaps motivate this work. First, most systems, including DDI-GPT and DDIAgents, op-
erate on drug pairs, whereas real polypharmacy commonly involves three or more medications.
HODDI [8] explicitly targets high-order interactions, but is a benchmark with GNN and hyper-
graph baselines rather than an end-to-end multi-drug prediction-and-explanation system.
    Second, locally hosted LLMs and chained multi-agent pipelines can be resource-intensive and
add substantial inference latency. This project instead uses a single API-based LLM over a retrieved
subgraph, prioritising simplicity and low latency while extending to multi-drug queries. Third,
retrieval alone can expose only interactions already represented in the graph; it cannot readily

1.3 Objective and Project Scope

1.3.1 Objective
Taking DDI-GPT as a baseline reference, this project develops an end-to-end clinical decision-
support framework that predicts and explains adverse eﬀects for regimens of three or more drugs.
The framework establishes an API-driven Graph-RAG pipeline over the Decagon biomedical graph.
Instead of a heavy locally hosted pairwise model, a lightweight API-based LLM orchestrator pro-
cesses multi-drug cocktails simultaneously and synthesises multi-hop biological pathways into clin-
ically interpretable rationales with minimal inference latency.
    For a queried drug set, the system retrieves a local subgraph containing the drugs, their target
proteins, protein–protein links among those targets, and known side-eﬀect edges. It serialises this
evidence into compact context, from which an API-based LLM predicts likely side eﬀects and
provides a natural-language explanation.

1.3.2 Dataset and suitability
The Decagon dataset from BioSNAP at Stanford is a standardised resource for polypharmacy side
eﬀects. It is provided as three connected CSV ﬁles:
• bio-decagon-targets.csv: human proteins targeted by approximately 645 drugs.
• bio-decagon-ppi.csv: physical interactions among approximately 19,000 human proteins.
• bio-decagon-combo.csv: observed side eﬀects, across 964 types, for co-prescribed drug pairs.
In full, the graph contains 645 drugs, 19,085 proteins, 18,596 drug–protein edges, 715,612 protein–
protein edges, and 4,651,131 drug–drug side-eﬀect edges. Standard identiﬁers connect the three
ﬁles without manual labelling or external scraping. The entities map directly to drug and protein
nodes, and the relations map to typed drug–protein, protein–protein, and drug–drug side-eﬀect
edges. This structure makes a compact local subgraph available for Graph-RAG.

1.3.3 Deliverable and boundaries
The deliverable is a functional, low-latency API pipeline that accepts multi-drug queries, dynami-
cally extracts a local subgraph, and returns qualitative side-eﬀect predictions with mechanistic bio-
logical rationales. Training a graph neural network to predict novel unobserved adverse edges is out-
side the current phase. Continuous clinical variables—dose, pharmacokinetics, and renal/hepatic
clearance—and empirical wet-lab validation are also future work.

Background
2.1 The Knowledge Graph

The biomedical knowledge graph contains two node types—drugs and proteins—and three relation
types (Table 2.1). The drug–drug relation is sourced from Decagon.


                        Table 2.1: Relation types in the knowledge graph
Relation                   Description
Drug → Protein             Proteins a drug binds to or modulates. This is a property of an individual drug
                           and does not depend on co-prescribed drugs.
Protein ↔ Protein          A ﬁxed, drug-independent network of physical protein interactions, including
                           signal transduction and shared pathways.
Drug ↔ Drug                Previously documented side eﬀects for speciﬁc drug pairs, as recorded in
                           Decagon.


   Only the third relation is inherently pairwise: each polypharmacy edge connects exactly two
drugs. Drug–target and protein–protein relations remain available for any number of drugs.

2.2 Standard Graph-RAG

Graph-RAG has two stages. During retrieval, the graph is traversed outward from entities named
in a query to collect a relevant neighbourhood, preserving relational structure rather than relying
only on textual similarity. During generation, that neighbourhood is serialised as text and supplied
to an LLM, allowing the model to reason over explicit structure rather than memorised associations.
Retrieval is not intrinsically limited to two entities because it can expand from any query set.

2.3 Problem Statement

Given a drug regimen of k ≥ 3 drugs, the task is to predict potential side eﬀects. Each prediction
is annotated with an interpretive tier and the supporting path in the retrieved subgraph.

    Bridges are restricted to at most two hops. Longer paths would introduce many indirectly
related proteins and make the neighbourhood noisy; the restriction keeps each bridge biologically
direct. Shared targets expose a signal unavailable to pairwise analysis: a protein targeted by three
drugs becomes visible only after their neighbourhoods are combined. Proteins targeted by exactly
two drugs remain in the neighbourhood and are covered by pairwise history. The assembled context
prompts the LLM to reason from target perturbation to possible adverse outcomes before returning
a ﬁnal answer.

3.2 Pruning Before Passing to the LLM

Housekeeping proteins can interact with very many proteins and ﬂood a neighbourhood with
mechanism-irrelevant information. They are ﬁltered before serialisation. A direct target of any
regimen drug is always retained; other proteins are retained only if their global protein-network
connectivity is below a chosen threshold. A protein above that threshold is treated as a generic
hub and removed together with bridges that depend on it.

    The model is constrained to a ﬁxed structured format (for example, JSON). For every predicted
side eﬀect it must state the assigned tier, a supporting subgraph path, and a plain-language expla-
nation. Each cited path is automatically checked against the retrieved subgraph; a prediction with
no valid path is discarded. Tier 2 and Tier 3 claims are thus traceable to retrieved structure and
machine-readable for downstream use. Tier 3 predictions remain hypotheses requiring experimental
or clinical validation.
